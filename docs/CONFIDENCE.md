# Calibrated confidence for visual Jev-style decisions

**Research date:** 2026-09-23

**Scope:** Open implementations of typed Choice/Noul/Score decisions, probability calibration, and the path from the current visual probe to trustworthy soccer-video decisions.
**Evidence status:** Source and published-result review. The external models and benchmarks below were not independently rerun in this workspace.

## Read this first

1. TypeSafe publishes the Jev response contract and says `confidence` summarizes the concentration of a Choice or Score probability distribution. It does **not** publish the exact statistic. Noul returns a probability of yes without a separate confidence field. [Confidence guide](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence), [API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api).
2. Distribution concentration, a model's normalized option score, and an empirically calibrated probability of correctness are three different quantities. None implies the others. A 0.90 action threshold must be validated against labeled examples from the intended workload.
3. Open projects now reproduce much of the typed decision interface by reading allowed answer logits directly, sometimes training a decision head, and then fitting a calibration map. The strongest comparative evidence found is for **text decisions**. It does not establish soccer-video accuracy or calibration.
4. The current [visual contract probe](experiments/README.md) uses Qwen3.5-2B option-token logits and a provisional normalized-entropy `confidence` in [modal_jev_contract.py](experiments/modal_jev_contract.py). Its four synthetic cases are a wiring check: both goal examples were labeled `miss`, and Score gave strong goal evidence to both video cases. Calibrating those scores alone cannot fix the wrong choices.

## What Jev exposes and what remains unknown

The public endpoint accepts `state`, `model`, and a map of typed `questions`. Choice returns a selected option and a distribution across criteria; Score returns a distribution across ordered levels and its probability-weighted expected level; Noul returns the probability of yes. Choice and Score also return `confidence` in `[0, 1]`. TypeSafe describes it as a statistic derived from distribution shape, rather than the top option's probability. The public docs do not specify a formula or prove that a particular confidence value equals a frequency of correct decisions on every downstream task. Jev currently accepts text, including structured JSON containing text, but no native image, audio, or video input. [API reference](https://docs.typesafe.ai/api), [confidence guide](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence), [state documentation](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/state).

For this project, retain separate internal names:

- **Option distribution** `p`: normalized scores over the declared answers. Before validation, call these *conditional option scores* or *uncalibrated probabilities*.
- **Concentration** `c(p)`: entropy, margin, or another scalar describing distribution shape. The current proxy is `1 - H(p)/log(K)`. It is not Jev's published formula and is not the probability the answer is correct.
- **Calibrated answer probability**: a probability map fitted on labeled examples and shown on untouched test examples to match observed outcomes. For a binary goal question, this is the desired `P(goal | clip, question)` estimate. For a multiclass choice, evaluate each class as well as top-label correctness.
- **Action rule**: a threshold, prediction set, or abstention policy chosen for a stated error budget. It is a separate decision from probability estimation.

Do not rename the current entropy proxy as calibrated confidence or use it to authorize automated actions.

## Algorithms to compare

There is no universal best calibrator. Select using a separate calibration split, then evaluate once on an untouched test split. Prefer proper scoring rules such as log loss and Brier score alongside reliability plots and ECE; a low ECE from a small or convenient binning scheme is insufficient. The [CalArena benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.30188) evaluated nearly 2,000 calibration experiments and found that smooth mappings generally outperform binning methods and that multiclass-specific methods matter in high-dimensional settings. Its [Probmetrics implementation](https://github.com/probkit/probmetrics) provides a common comparison framework.

| Method | Best starting use | Main tradeoff |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Temperature scaling** `softmax(z/T)` | First baseline when full candidate logits are available; one scalar fitted by held-out log loss. [Guo et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04599) | Preserves option ranking; cannot fix missed goals or a biased decision boundary. One value may not transfer across questions, models, languages, or visual domains. |
| **Sigmoid/Platt scaling** | Binary goal detection, especially when the raw scorer needs a slope **and intercept**. [scikit-learn guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.calibration.CalibratedClassifierCV) | Can move a binary decision boundary, but still needs representative labels and testing under the deployment class balance. |
| **Isotonic regression or Venn–Abers** | Binary tasks with a large enough calibration set and a nonlinear score-to-outcome relationship. [scikit-learn guide](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.calibration.CalibratedClassifierCV), [Venn–Abers code](https://github.com/ip200/venn-abers) | More flexible and easier to overfit with few examples; scikit-learn cautions against isotonic when calibration data are much smaller than 1,000 examples. |
| **Regularized multiclass scaling** | Choice/Score distributions when one temperature leaves class-specific errors. Compare [structured matrix scaling](https://github.com/probkit/probmetrics) and [Dirichlet calibration](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12656). | More parameters require more calibration data and regularization. Preserve the exact criterion names and candidate count in evaluation. |
| **Conformal prediction: LAC, APS, RAPS** | A prediction **set** with a target marginal coverage, or an abstention workflow. [MAPIE theory and implementations](https://mapie.readthedocs.io/en/stable/content/conformal-prediction/classification/) | Coverage needs exchangeable calibration/test examples and is marginal, not a guaranteed correctness probability for one clip or each subgroup. |

Margin and normalized entropy can remain useful *ranking features* for uncertainty, but a sharper distribution need not be a more correct decision. Verbal requests for a model to state its own confidence should also be treated as a separate baseline, not as calibration.

## Recommended VL JEV experiment

1. **Define the visual judgment precisely.** Decide whether the target is a goal event, ball crossing the line, shot on target, or broadcast-confirmed goal. A still image can support visibility judgments but cannot, on its own, establish a temporal crossing. Record the event timestamp and which visual/audio cues are permitted.
2. **Build a representative, labeled broadcast set.** Include confirmed goals and hard negatives: near misses, saves, offside or disallowed goals, replay footage, obstructed ball, cuts before the outcome, and clips without enough evidence. Group train, calibration, and test splits by match; evaluate shifts by broadcast source, camera view, league, and clip quality. Treat missing evidence explicitly rather than forcing `goal`/`miss`.
3. **Improve the base event scorer before calibration.** Compare the current Qwen3.5-2B video option-logit method against a temporal soccer action-spotting baseline from [SoccerNet](https://github.com/SoccerNet/sn-spotting). The SoccerNet benchmark includes the Goal action and timestamped spotting protocols. Image-oriented decider and djev variants are useful image baselines, not evidence of temporal goal recognition. [SoccerNet action spotting](https://www.soccer-net.org/tasks/action-spotting).
4. **Keep raw scores and exact provenance.** Save candidate logits, option order, prompt, model/checkpoint revision, sampling frames and times, labels, match ID, and any missing-media or preprocessing failures. Do not silently replace missing scores with confident values.
5. **Calibrate each stable model and question definition.** Begin with temperature scaling. Compare Platt scaling for a binary goal decision; compare richer methods only if the calibration split is sufficiently large and the simple fit leaves consistent errors. Fit solely on the calibration split. Decide the threshold there, then evaluate both probability quality and action outcomes on the untouched test split.
6. **Report decision quality and probability quality separately.** For event spotting, include timestamp tolerance, precision/recall and false positives; for clip classification, include confusion matrix and per-class results. Report Brier score, log loss, reliability plots, ECE with bin counts, and accuracy/error versus coverage when abstaining. Show confidence intervals and subgroup results. Never use four synthetic probe cases as an accuracy or calibration estimate.
7. **Specify the action policy last.** Choose a maximum acceptable false-goal or missed-goal rate and measure which clips can be automated at that bound. Do not inherit TypeSafe's illustrative `0.5`/`0.9` thresholds: its own guide says thresholds depend on the domain and must be tested on local data. [TypeSafe confidence guide](https://docs.typesafe.ai/confidence).

**Current recommendation:** Keep the existing response shape for experimentation, but document its `confidence` as a provisional concentration statistic. Do not claim Jev-equivalent or calibrated confidence until a held-out broadcast evaluation supports it. Improving temporal recognition has priority because a post-hoc temperature cannot turn the current false `miss` decisions into correct `goal` decisions.
